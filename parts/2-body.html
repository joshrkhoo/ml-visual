Fitting a line by gradient descent

Each weight vector \(\mathbf w=[w_1,w_2]\) is one line on the left and one point on the right. Gradient descent walks downhill on the right; the line on the left follows. Drag the data, or drag the point on the loss surface.

\(\displaystyle f_{\mathbf w}(x)=\mathbf w\cdot\phi(x),\;\; \phi(x)=[1,x]\)\(\displaystyle \text{TrainLoss}(\mathbf w)=\frac1{|D|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D}\big(\mathbf w\cdot\phi(x)-y\big)^2\)\(\displaystyle \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta\,\underbrace{\frac{2}{|D|}\sum\big(\mathbf w\cdot\phi(x)-y\big)\phi(x)}_{\nabla_{\mathbf w}\text{TrainLoss}}\)
Lecture 2, slides 8–15. The lecture's data \((1,1),(2,3),(4,3)\) with \(\mathbf w=[1,0.57]\) gives TrainLoss \(=0.38\).

Score, margin and the hinge loss

The classifier is \(\text{sign}(\mathbf w\cdot\phi(x))\), so \(\mathbf w\) points into the +1 side and the boundary is perpendicular to it. Drag the tip of \(\mathbf w\). The right plot puts every example at its margin: the zero-one loss is flat there, so only hinge and logistic give gradient descent anything to follow.

\(\displaystyle \text{score}=\mathbf w\cdot\phi(x)\)\(\displaystyle \text{margin}=(\mathbf w\cdot\phi(x))\,y\)\(\displaystyle \text{Loss}_{0\text{-}1}=\mathbf 1[\text{margin}\le 0]\)\(\displaystyle \text{Loss}_{\text{hinge}}=\max\{1-\text{margin},0\}\)\(\displaystyle \text{Loss}_{\text{logistic}}=\log(1+e^{-\text{margin}})\)
Lecture 2, slides 17–27, with \(\phi(x)=[x_1,x_2]\) and the training set \(([0,2],+1),([-2,0],+1),([1,-1],-1)\).

Overfitting and the bias–variance tradeoff

Fit polynomials of growing degree to noisy samples of \(\sin 2\pi x\). The faint curves are fits to other training sets drawn from the same distribution: their spread is the variance, and the gap between their average and the truth is the bias.

\(\displaystyle \text{error}_S(h)=\frac1n\sum_{x\in S}\big(h(x)-y\big)^2\)\(\displaystyle \text{error}_{\mathcal D}(h)=\mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D}\big(h(x)-y\big)^2\)\(\displaystyle \mathbb E\,\text{error}_{\mathcal D}=\underbrace{(\bar h-f)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb E(h-\bar h)^2}_{\text{variance}}+\sigma^2\)
Lecture 3, slides 3–9. \(h\) overfits when another \(h'\) has larger training error but smaller error over \(\mathcal D\).

k-fold cross-validation

The \(m\) examples are split into \(k\) folds. Each round trains on \(k-1\) folds and validates on the one left out, so every example is validated exactly once. Click a round to see its split; the averaged validation error picks the degree.

\(\displaystyle \text{CV}_k(d)=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\text{error}_{\text{fold }i}\big(h_d^{(-i)}\big)\)\(\displaystyle \text{training runs}=k\times(\text{number of models})\)\(\displaystyle k=m\;\Rightarrow\;\text{leave-one-out}\)
Lecture 3, slides 10–13. Each example trains in \(k-1\) rounds, so a fraction \(1-\tfrac1k\) of the data is used for training each time.

The perceptron learning rule

Examples are visited in turn. A correct prediction leaves the weights alone; a mistake moves them by \(\alpha I (T-O)\), which tilts and shifts the boundary toward the right answer. The default run reproduces the lecture's trace and stops at \(\mathbf w=(1,-3,4)\).

\(\displaystyle O=\text{step}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^n w_jI_j\Big),\;\; I_0=1,\;w_0=-\theta\)\(\displaystyle w_j\leftarrow w_j+\alpha\,I_j\,(T-O)\;\;\forall j\)
Lecture 4 (perceptron), slides 6–22. If the examples are linearly separable the rule converges in finitely many steps from any start.

AND, OR, NOT, and why XOR needs a hidden unit

A single perceptron draws one line, so it can only represent linearly separable functions. Set the weights by hand or let the rule learn them. XOR has no separating line; the lecture's two-layer network solves it by feeding a hidden AND unit into the output with weight −2.

\(\displaystyle O=\text{step}(w_1I_1+w_2I_2-\theta)\)\(\displaystyle y_3=\text{step}(x_1+x_2-1.5)\)\(\displaystyle y=\text{step}(x_1+x_2-2y_3-0.5)\)
Lecture 4 (perceptron), slides 8–14, and Lecture 4 (MLP), slide 2.

Learning rate, batch GD and SGD

An Adaline is a linear unit, so its error surface is a bowl with one global minimum. A small η creeps down the valley; a large η zig-zags across it, and past \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) it diverges. SGD uses one example per update: cheap and noisy. Mini-batches sit in between.

\(\displaystyle o=\sum_{i=0}^n w_ix_i\)\(\displaystyle E(\mathbf w)=\frac1{2N}\sum_{d}(t_d-o_d)^2\)\(\displaystyle \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta\,\nabla_{\!B}E,\quad \nabla_{\!B}E=-\frac1{|B|}\sum_{d\in B}(t_d-o_d)\,\mathbf x_d\)
Lecture 4 (Adaline), slides 2–9. Batch: \(B\) = all \(N\) examples. SGD: \(|B|=1\). Mini-batch: \(|B|=b\).

Activation functions and their gradients

Backprop needs a slope. The step function has none, the sigmoid's peaks at 0.25 and fades on both sides, and ReLU passes gradient 1 or 0. On the right, one hidden unit's response over the input plane: a sigmoid unit responds over a whole half-plane, an RBF unit only near its centre.

\(\displaystyle \sigma(x)=\frac1{1+e^{-x}},\;\; \sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\)\(\displaystyle \tanh'=1-\tanh^2\)\(\displaystyle \text{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x)\)\(\displaystyle \text{RBF: }\exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert\mathbf x-\mathbf w_j\rVert^2}{2\sigma_j^2}\Big)\)
Lecture 4 (MLP), slides 5–9. With linear hidden units, \(W_2W_1\mathbf x=(W_2W_1)\mathbf x\): extra layers add nothing.

Backpropagation on a computation graph

Forward: values flow left to right in topological order. Backward: each node multiplies its local gradient by the gradient arriving from above and passes the product down. Step through it and watch the add, mul and max gates.

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}}_{\text{local}}\;\underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}}_{\text{upstream}}\)\(\text{add: distributes}\)\(\text{max: routes}\)\(\text{mul: switches}\)\(\displaystyle \frac{d\sigma}{dx}=\sigma(1-\sigma)\)
Lecture 4 (computation graphs), slides 1–40. Gradients add where a value branches.

Momentum and local minima

A network's error surface can have several minima, and gradient descent stops in whichever basin it starts in. Momentum gives each weight inertia, which speeds it along valleys and can carry it over a shallow ridge. Click the surface to choose a start; the two paths share it.

\(\displaystyle \Delta\mathbf w(t+1)=-\eta\,\nabla E\big(\mathbf w(t)\big)+\alpha\,\Delta\mathbf w(t),\)\(\displaystyle 0\lt\alpha\lt 1\)
Lecture 4 (gradient descent), slides 11–13. \(\alpha=0\) is plain gradient descent.

Training an MLP

A small multi-layer perceptron trained by backprop with momentum, in the spirit of the TensorFlow Playground demo. Watch the hidden units' lines combine into a curved boundary. One hidden unit can only draw a line; XOR needs at least two.

\(\displaystyle \delta_k=(o_k-t_k)\,o_k(1-o_k)\)\(\displaystyle \delta_j=g'(\text{in}_j)\sum_k W_{k,j}\,\delta_k\)\(\displaystyle \Delta W_{j,i}(t+1)=-\eta\,\delta_j\,a_i+\alpha\,\Delta W_{j,i}(t)\)
Lecture 4 (MLP and gradient descent). Sigmoid output, squared error \(\tfrac12(t-o)^2\), weights start at small random values.

Universal approximation

One hidden layer of sigmoids, each a soft step at its own position, can build any well-behaved function: the output layer scales the steps and adds them up. Add units and the staircase hugs the target more closely.

\(\displaystyle \hat f(x)=b+\sum_{j=1}^{H}v_j\,\sigma\big(k(x-c_j)\big)\)\(\displaystyle \max_x\lvert\hat f(x)-f(x)\rvert\to 0\ \text{ as } H\to\infty\)
Lecture 4 (MLP), slides 10–11. Here the hidden steps are spread evenly over the interval and the output weights \(v_j\) are fitted by least squares.

Convolution: flip, slide, multiply, add

To get output \(m\), flip the kernel, slide it so it sits over position \(m\), multiply overlapping entries and add. Turn off the flip and you have cross-correlation, which is what CNN libraries actually compute.

\(\displaystyle (f*g)(m)=\sum_{n}f(n)\,g(m-n)\)\(\displaystyle \text{cross-correlation: }\sum_n f(m+n)\,g(n)\)\(\displaystyle f*g=g*f\)
Lecture 5 (image processing basics), slides 3–6.

Padding and stride

Without padding every convolution shrinks the map by \(k-1\), which limits how deep the network can go. Zero-padding keeps the size; a stride of 2 skips every other position and halves it.

\(\displaystyle n_{\text{out}}=\Big\lfloor\frac{n+2p-k}{s}\Big\rfloor+1\)\(\displaystyle p=\frac{k-1}{2},\;s=1\;\Rightarrow\;n_{\text{out}}=n\)
Lecture 5 (CNN), slides 13–19.

Max pooling and small shifts

Once a feature is found, only its rough position matters. Max pooling keeps the strongest response in each window, so nudging the input by a pixel often leaves the pooled map unchanged. Shift the input and compare.

\(\displaystyle P(i,j)=\max_{0\le a,b\lt q}\;F(si+a,\;sj+b)\)\(\displaystyle \text{size }q=2,\ \text{stride }s=2\ \Rightarrow\ \text{map halves}\)
Lecture 5 (CNN), slides 15–17. Average pooling replaces the max with the mean.

Receptive fields and parameter sharing

Each unit sees only a small window of the layer below, yet the window it covers in the input grows with every layer. And because one kernel is reused at every position, a convolution needs far fewer weights than a fully connected layer.

\(\displaystyle r_\ell=r_{\ell-1}+(k-1)\prod_{i\lt\ell}s_i,\;\; r_0=1\)\(\displaystyle \text{FC: }(n^2C_{\text{in}})(n^2C_{\text{out}})\)\(\displaystyle \text{local: }n^2C_{\text{out}}(k^2C_{\text{in}}+1)\)\(\displaystyle \text{conv: }C_{\text{out}}(k^2C_{\text{in}}+1)\)
Lecture 5 (CNN), slides 2–11. Parameter counts include biases; same-size output assumed.
